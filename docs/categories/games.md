# Games

What "good" means for a Games plan, and what is Games' own in code. Shared rules are in [principles.md](../product/principles.md); the runtime every category shares is in [launch-runtime.md](../backend/launch-runtime.md). Film and Music are the models for this one. Behaviour and numbers are in the [spec](../product/spec.md): the query catalog (§4.4), the tests and §6.1 the modules.

## What a launch plan has to settle

A plan is a set of decisions, not a document:

| Decision | What it covers |
|---|---|
| Where | The regions to lead with and in what order: where the game's players are strongest, and where the spend and the community work go |
| When | The release date or window against the calendar of its platforms, and every beat before it |
| Who | Core and secondary audience, what else they play, watch, listen to and buy |
| Which collabs | The fan bases to approach in each kind of media: artists, series, anime, films, brands, podcasts and other games. A collab the team already has in mind is ranked beside them |
| Store and positioning | The platforms, the store tags and the themes of the trailer and key art, tied to what the comparable games carry |
| How to reach them | Creators and streamers, gaming podcasts and press, communities, brand partners |
| Cost and measures | A budget split and the numbers to track for this kind of launch |
| Risks | A platform the comparable games are not on, tags the store will not match, a region that converts softly, a date that slips |

## Launch kinds

A game is launched in one of four ways, and each has its own calendar, its own sections and its own measures. The agent reads the kind from the brief. When the brief does not say, it plans a premium PC launch and says so in "To sharpen this plan".

| Kind | Sections it gets, in order | The calendar | What to track |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Premium** (PC and console) | Store page and tags, Festivals and showcases, Fan base collabs, Creators and streamers, Press and podcasts, Brand partners, Community | Store page and wishlist drive from a year out, a demo and a festival, console certification, preview keys, the date lock, launch keys, launch week, the first patch and the first discount | Wishlists by region, wishlist to purchase in launch week, review score in the first 72 hours, day 7 retention |
| **Early access** | Store page and tags, Early access roadmap, Festivals and showcases, Community, Creators and streamers, Fan base collabs, Press and podcasts | As premium, plus a public roadmap, an update every four to six weeks and 1.0 planned as a second launch | As premium, plus the review trend after each update |
| **Free to play and live service** | Fan base collabs, Creators and streamers, Seasons and retention, Community, Festivals and showcases, Store page and tags, Brand partners, Press and podcasts | A closed beta, pre-registration, an open beta, season one and a season every eight to twelve weeks, each with a collab beat | Beta sign-ups by region, day 1, 7 and 30 retention, payer conversion, season engagement |
| **Mobile** | Soft launch, Store page and tags, Fan base collabs, Creators and streamers, Seasons and retention, Community, Brand partners | Soft launch in test markets, a gate on retention and cost per install, pre-registration, a featuring nomination, global launch and live events | Pre-registrations by country, retention by soft-launch market, cost per install, store conversion |

The platforms shape the calendar inside a kind. Festivals and showcases need a PC or console release. A soft launch is for iOS and Android. Console certification and an age rating appear only for a console release, and the Steam rules (the Coming Soon page, the date lock, the festival rules and the discount cooldown) only for PC. Every plan also has Regions, Launch beats, Budget split and What to measure.

## Requirements 

1. **Plan for the team's decisions.** Their regions, platforms, launch kind, age band, comparable games, store tags, collabs and window are constraints. Test them and say what Qloo found, never replace them. A platform the comparable games are not on, or tags the store will not match, are said plainly, and the team's choice stays beside the stronger one. Every named region, comparable game and collab gets an answer.
2. **Work with thin input.** A one-line description, such as "a Nigerian mobile shooter", is enough. Ask for nothing beyond what the game is, and never ask for comparable games. With no region named, plan for the usual markets of this launch kind; a nationality ("built for Nigerians") names the region. With no platform named, assume the kind's usual platforms. End with what would sharpen the plan. A name alone, with no sense of what the game is and nowhere to launch, gets one welcoming card asking for both; nothing is guessed.
3. **Describe the audience with games like it, so the person need not.** Comparable games the person names lead. Fewer than three, and the analyst works out who the players are from the description and chooses comparable games for them, each with its reason and labelled as chosen by Oraqul. With no model, or when none resolves, the games Qloo lists for the genre stand in, at lower confidence. A game Qloo finds by exact name leads the audience question. A debut it does not know is planned from the comparable games, and the plan says so. The franchise and earlier games by the studio anchor the audience like a comparable game and are not listed as one.
4. **Cover what Qloo cannot measure from knowledge.** When the brief says who the game is built for by language, culture or community, Qloo cannot target it and never receives it (only public entity names, place names and age bands go to Qloo). The model covers it as labelled playbook lines: the language of the store page, trailer and text, the creators and communities that reach that audience, the devices, app stores, data costs and payment habits of that market, and local brand partners. When search is configured, the web step also looks up how players in the named regions get and pay for games, and a line may state such a fact only with its source. The plan never claims what that audience likes without a finding, and never stereotypes.
5. **Be date-aware.** Beats are dates when the release date is known, and weeks before it when not. A beat that is already over is never advised. A festival, sale or showcase date appears only when a web source states it, with the source. If the release date has passed, the plan says there is nothing left to plan and points to an audit.
6. **Be realistic about scale.** An independent studio gets in-kind support from a brand, not sponsorship cash. Who will partner with a studio and what the audience likes are different questions, and Qloo answers only the second. Code answers the second from the budget and who is launching (a solo or small launch is not offered a national brand, and the brands Qloo finds for the audience are offered only when the launch can reach them), read from the brief and never guessed.
7. **Be actionable.** Each line is the move, the reason and who does it. Readable in about five minutes, short lines, plain words, no data jargon in the plan. People who play the game are players.
8. **Never invent the game.** Its story, characters, mechanics and features come only from the brief. What the audience plays is what to speak to, never what the game contains.
9. **No forecasts.** No wishlist, sales, player or review predictions, and no pricing advice.
10. **Be exportable and revisable.** PDF, Word and Markdown built as documents; a what-if reruns the plan, such as adding a collab, a region or a comparable game.

## What grounded means for Games

- **Examples.** A plain model names the biggest games, bands and brands. A grounded plan names the actual artists, series, anime, films, brands, podcasts and games this audience ranks highest, kind of media by kind of media.
- **Fan bases are ranked within a kind of media, never across.** Qloo scores one kind of entity at a time, so how close music is to the players cannot be compared with how close a podcast is. The bridge shows the best in each kind, and a collab the team named is scored in the same call as the rest of its kind, so it shows where it stands among them.
- **The team's assumptions are tested.** The age band, each named region and city, the platforms and the store tags are checked against Qloo's data on the comparable games. A platform none of them is on, or a set of tags none of them carry, is told so.
- **Where a chatbot beats us.** Qloo has no wishlists, sales, reviews, festival dates, store rules or creator data, so the calendar, the festival, certification and soft launch advice, and the creators section come from launch knowledge, labelled as such. We win on who the audience is and what else it follows in each kind of media.

The plan text never uses the word "overlap" (the writer is told never to use it); the Fan base bridge tab does, because there it is the measure on the bar.

## Known limits

- **Game signals are mostly verified.** A game works as a signal for music, series, films, brands, podcasts, games, people and regions (`qloo:games-probes`, recorded in [qloo.md](../backend/qloo.md)). Anime and the genre-only fallback are not yet, and the plan is not claimed to beat a chatbot on them.
- **Creators and streamers are launch practice.** Qloo may not hold them. Until the probe says it does, the Creators section is playbook, and the Assumptions tab says creators were not measured.
- **A debut is planned from its comparable games.** If those are off, the audience is off. The plan says what it rests on.
- **Platform and store rules are launch knowledge.** The Steam and console rules in the calendar come from the platforms' own documentation and can change. Check them before a date is announced.
- **Festival, sale and showcase dates change every year.** They come from web research when search is configured, and are otherwise left out of the plan.
- **Regions are cities.** A region such as Europe is ranked through the cities in Qloo's table that stand for it. A region the table does not cover is not ranked, and the plan says so.
- **The bridge shows what the audience already follows.** Its candidates are the audience's own top picks in each kind of media, so a high overlap says "close to the players", not "will say yes". Anime is often empty: Qloo returned no anime for a game audience in the probe, and Gaps says so.
- **Overlap is not a share of players.** It is how close another audience is to the players, scored by Qloo, on a scale of 0 to 100.
- **Ambiguous names** are settled in code and the plan says which was chosen. Game and franchise names collide often. A wrong match is possible.

## Launch rules

Thresholds and bands are in [launch-rules.md](../product/launch-rules.md). For Games:

* **Calendar.** About 85 percent of wishlists are collected at least four months before release, so the store page and the wishlist drive start by 16 weeks out at the latest. The beats are the launch kind's (`games/modules.ts`).
* **Signal.** Wishlists, with a benchmark target of 7,000 and a floor of 5,000 where the team gives none. Followers, views and demo plays are secondary.
* **Channels.** YouTube leads, then TikTok and Discord.
* **Territories.** One plan per named region, home first, beside the regions ranking.

## How it works in code

### The one idea

A game is described by the **game** itself when Qloo knows it by name, by its **franchise** (earlier games, or by the same studio), and by up to four **comparable games**. The agent asks Qloo what that audience likes, kind of media by kind of media, because the question a games team asks is which fan bases to collaborate with and which regions to lead with. The game is an anchor but never a comp. A debut that Qloo does not know is planned from the comparable games, and the plan says so.

The two stages are Film's: code runs the queries and turns each result into a finding, then the model writes the plan from the findings and code checks every line. Games adds one thing the other two do not have: a **launch kind** (premium, early access, free to play, mobile) that decides the sections, the calendar, the measures and the budget split.

### What is shared and what is Games'

`apps/api/src/agent/launch/` is the runtime; `apps/api/src/agent/games/` is what Games adds, wired together in `games/config.ts` (`gamesLaunch`, a `LaunchConfig`).

### Where Games differs from Film and Music

**Anchors.** `findComps` looks the game up with the comps (`config.lead`). The franchise joins the lookup through `config.teamAnchors`: it anchors the audience question like the comparable games, after the named ones so it never crowds one out, and counts toward the three a plan wants.

**Thin briefs.** The analyst (`launch/analyse.ts`) fills `suggestedComps` from the description when the person named fewer than three; there is no hardcoded list. With no model, or when none resolves, the genre's own games from Qloo stand in, and the build does not stop to ask for comps. A nationality in the brief becomes a region, which `REGION_CITIES` turns into cities.

**What Qloo cannot measure.** The reader keeps `audienceNote`, the language, culture or community the game is built for, in the person's words (quote-checked). It goes to the writer, the query planner and the web search, never to a Qloo call. The writer is told to cover it from its own knowledge as playbook lines, and the web step searches how players in the named regions get and pay for games. The planner may also name local brands for any ranked region (`askCities`), which Qloo then ranks for the audience.

**The launch kind.** The reader takes it from the brief (a model's choice kept only with its quote, or code's rule over the words: early access first, then mobile, then free to play). With nothing said, `kindOf` returns premium with `assumed` true, and "To sharpen this plan" says what was assumed. The kind picks the modules in order (`KIND_PROFILES[kind].modules`), the beats, the measures and the budget split (`packages/packs/src/games/modules.ts`). The platforms then remove what does not apply: festivals need a PC or console, a soft launch needs iOS or Android.

**The calendar is written by code.** `config.codeSections.timeline` builds Launch beats from the kind and the platforms with `beatsFor` and `datedBeats`. With a release date (a day in the window), each beat has dates; without one, weeks. A beat that is over on `today` is dropped, so no plan advises around a past date, and a release date in the past gives one line that says there is nothing left to plan. The writer is not asked for the timeline, and its other sections cite web sources for festival and sale dates. `WriteInput.today` carries the date.

**Regions.** A region word (Europe, Southeast Asia) stands for table cities (`REGION_CITIES`). Those cities are ranked in the same heatmap call but never become the team's own assumptions: only a city the person named does. With no region named, the kind's usual markets are ranked and shown as the plan's regions (`standInPlaces`), and the text says they are the regions a launch like this usually checks. A region word the table has no cities for is a gap.

**The fan base bridge** (`fanBases` in `games/findings.ts`). For each of the seven media (music, series, anime, film, brand, podcast, game) it asks `findTaste` for what the anchors' audience likes and ranks the top four by `affinity × 100`. Qloo gives the same affinity open as scored, so a medium with no collab costs one call. With a collab of that medium, one `scoreRivals` call of that type scores the collab with the rest, without leave one out, and its confidence is capped at medium. The rows go to `brief.fan` grouped by medium and carry a `medium`. Because Qloo scores one entity type per call, a row is compared with its own medium only. A collab is found by name across the media with `searchEntity` and belongs to the medium of its entity type; several exact matches are not guessed. Anime is asked first so the series list does not repeat it. `before` and `after` are equal: a collab is a ranked row, not a moved bar.

**Two tests only Games has.** `gameProfiles` reads what Qloo lists for the comparable games (`/entities`). The platforms the team named are tested against how many of them list each (`platformVerdict`), and the store tags against the tags at least half of them share (`storeTagVerdict`). They are the assumptions `a-platform` and `a-store-tags` with findings of the same names.

**Creators.** `CREATORS_GROUNDED` in `packages/packs/src/games/catalog.ts` is false until the creators probe says Qloo returns streamers for a game signal and [qloo.md](../backend/qloo.md) records it. While false, the Creators section is playbook, and the gap says creators were not measured.

**Audit.** The shared tests (age, gender, place, comps, partner, window) plus the platforms, the store tags, each named collab (its rank among the partners scored in its medium, by thirds) and each named creator when creators are grounded. The results the person adds are by region, compared as in Film. A weak or broken platform, tag or collab keeps the team's choice and offers what to add beside it.

| To change | Edit |
|---|---|
| A threshold or formula | spec §5 first, then `engine/verdicts.ts` or `scoring.ts` |
| What the reader pulls from a brief | `GAMES_READ_SYSTEM` and `gamesReadingSchema` in `games/read.ts` |
| Which queries exist, and their defaults | spec §4.3, then `packages/packs/src/games/catalog.ts` and `games/findings.ts` |
